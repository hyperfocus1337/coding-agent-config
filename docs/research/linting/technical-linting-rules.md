# Consensus rules for technical documentation: a linter-ready rule set for AI-written docs

Google, Microsoft, and GitLab agree on most of the rules that matter: sentence-case headings, second person, active voice, present tense, descriptive link text, fenced and language-tagged code, parallel lists, the serial comma, no Latin abbreviations, and spelled-out numbers from zero through nine. Their main split is punctuation. Google and Microsoft allow unspaced em dashes, while GitLab bans em dashes, en dashes, and semicolons outright.\[1\]\[2\]\[3\] For docs written by AI agents, adopt GitLab's stricter position as your default.

## TL;DR

- **Consensus is strong and mostly checkable.** Heading hierarchy, sentence case, heading punctuation, code-fence language tags, list markup, link text, Latin abbreviations, "please", exclamation marks, and dash characters can all be enforced with regex or Markdown AST checks. Tone, list-versus-prose choices, and document type need heuristics and should never block a commit.
- **Where the guides conflict, pick the stricter option.** Ban em and en dashes (GitLab), allow common contractions but not "let's" or noun-plus-verb forms (Google plus Microsoft), use `month day, year` dates in prose and ISO 8601 for numeric dates, and choose one placeholder style per repository.
- **AI-writing tells are real, but they are statistical.** Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing", excess-vocabulary research (Kobak et al.), and several Vale packages document them. Make the unambiguous artifacts errors (em dashes, emoji in headings, chatbot phrases). Make vocabulary lists warnings, and make structural patterns such as the rule of three suggestions, because any single signal also turns up in human writing.

## Key findings

- **Codified rules follow a three-tier severity model you can copy.** GitLab's Vale setup uses **error** "for branding guidelines, trademark guidelines, and anything that causes content on the documentation site to render incorrectly," **warning** "for general style guide rules, tenets, and best practices," and **suggestion** for "style preferences that may require refactoring." Only errors fail CI, and GitLab's Git hook reports errors only.\[4\]\[5\] That split maps cleanly onto agent hooks (errors block), pre-commit (errors block, warnings print), and CI (errors fail, warnings annotate the diff).
- **GitLab is the best evidence of what can be enforced.** Its style guide names the Vale rule that tests many of its rules: `British.yml`, `SelfReferential.yml`, `OxfordComma.yml`, `SentenceSpacing.yml`, `NonStandardQuotes.yml`, `MultiLineLinks.yml`, `ReferenceLinks.yml`, `Uppercase.yml`, and a Flesch-Kincaid `ReadingLevel.yml`. Markdown structure is enforced separately through markdownlint.\[1\]\[5\]
- **GitLab's own guidance on adding rules is worth adopting.** An error-level rule must start with zero existing violations. Too-broad or too-subjective rules create noise, and a rule that needs page refactoring belongs at suggestion level.\[5\]\[6\]
- **The Vale community already ships AI-tell rule sets.** These include `vale-ai-tells` (several forks, plus a separate Krishna Sunkam package with seventeen rules: six mechanical, eleven judgment calls), `vale-signs-of-ai-writing` from ammil industries (built from Wikipedia's page with error, warning, and suggestion tiers), and Vale's own "Voices" package. They all say they detect lexical patterns, not authorship.\[7\]\[8\]\[9\]\[10\]\[11\]

**Where they really differ.** The em dash is the only substantive conflict that touches AI habits. Google and Microsoft both treat the em dash as legitimate punctuation, and Microsoft warns only against overuse.\[2\]\[3\] GitLab bans it: "Use separate sentences, or commas, instead."\[1\] For agent-written docs, a total ban is the only version a linter can enforce without judgment calls. Wikipedia's editors also observe that AI-generated em dashes "are usually surrounded by spaces, contrary to common typographic guidelines."\[32\] So even under Google or Microsoft rules, a spaced em dash is already an error. Other minor differences:

- **"Let's":** Microsoft endorses it and Google discourages it.\[14\]\[18\] Default to forbidding it, because "let's" also opens the "Let's dive in" pattern.
- **Contractions in reference docs:** GitLab disallows them there.\[1\] Treat this as optional per document type.
- **Placeholders:** styles differ (`UPPER_SNAKE` versus `<angle>`), and consistency matters more than which one you pick.

## Structural rules

Each rule lists its rationale, support, how it can be checked, and a severity. "Regex" means a line-level pattern. "AST" means a check on the parsed Markdown, HTML, or Org tree. "Heuristic" means a scored or thresholded check with false positives. "Judgment" means a human or LLM review.

**Docstring mood conflict.** PEP 257 prescribes the imperative ("Return"), while Google's developer style guide examples use third person ("Returns a bird"),\[45\] and a python.org discussion proposes loosening PEP 257 to match.\[46\] Recommended default: follow the language's own convention (imperative for Python, name-first third person for Go, third person for Java and TypeScript). Enforce consistency within each language rather than one mood everywhere.

## Catching AI and LLM writing habits

### What the evidence says

- **Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing"** (WikiProject AI Cleanup) is the most-cited catalog. It reports that LLMs use em dashes "more often than nonprofessional human-written text of the same genre" and "in a formulaic, pat way," and that "LLMs overuse the rule of three." It also says that "AI-generated em dashes are usually surrounded by spaces." It warns that this "sign is most useful when taken in combination with other indicators," and notes that some vendors (it names OpenAI's GPT-5.1) now suppress em dashes.\[32\] Secondary summaries of the page add bold-lead bullet lists, excessive boldface, "it's important to note", and "in conclusion".\[56\]\[57\]\[58\] The page itself says it is "not a ban" and that punctuation signs "may not apply in a non-Wikipedia context."\[59\]
- **Excess-vocabulary research** gives the lexical list a quantitative base. Kobak, González-Márquez, Horvát, and Lause (Science Advances, 2025) studied "vocabulary changes in more than 15 million biomedical abstracts from 2010 to 2024 indexed by PubMed." They found words with strong excess usage in 2024, including "delves" (excess ratio r = 28.0), "underscores" (13.8), and "showcasing" (10.7). Their excess-word analysis "suggests that at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were processed with LLMs," with the lower bound "reaching 40% for some subcorpora." Juzek and Ward (COLING 2025) examine why ChatGPT overuses "delve", and Liang et al. (Nature Human Behaviour, 2025) quantify LLM use in scientific papers.\[60\] This is academic prose, not docs, so treat the word list as indicative.
- **Vale packages** turn these signs into rules:
  - `vale-ai-tells` flags vocabulary ("delve", "tapestry", "multifaceted", "leverage", "foster", "underscores", "comprehensive", "robust"), openers ("In today's rapidly evolving..."), hedges ("It's important to note"), em dashes, and sycophantic openers ("Great question!"). It documents two limits: Vale cannot detect sentence-length uniformity (its README says "AI produces sentences of near-uniform length, roughly 27 words, while human writing varies widely"), and Vale strips inline code before matching, so patterns whose subject is a backticked identifier fail silently.
  - The ammil industries ruleset tiers its rules as errors for "definite AI artifacts" (chatbot phrases, citation glitches like `:contentReference[oaicite:0]`, placeholders, tracking URLs), warnings for "likely AI patterns" (hedging clusters, knowledge-cutoff references), and suggestions for patterns "common in human writing."\[11\]
- **Caution on sources.** Several "AI tell" articles are marketing for humanizer tools. The claim that models have stopped using em dashes and that the "not X, it's Y" construction is now the strongest tell comes from blog commentary, not measurement.\[61\] Wikipedia's page changes daily, so pin the rules you derive from it to a date.\[11\]

**How to deploy these without crying wolf.** Run AI-1 through AI-6, AI-10, and AI-22 as blocking errors in the agent hook, because they have near-zero false positives and are cheap to fix. Show AI-7 through AI-20 to the agent as warnings in the same hook, since agents fix warnings reliably when told the rule. Humans should see them only in CI annotations. Keep AI-13, AI-16, AI-17, and AI-21 at suggestion level and review them in aggregate, because each has false positives on legitimate human prose. Wikipedia's editors themselves say "none of these signs alone proves" AI authorship.\[58\]

### Rollout advice

- Adopt GitLab's rule-promotion process. Start any new rule at warning level, fix existing violations, and then promote it to error. Don't ship an error rule that the current corpus violates.\[5\]
- Give agents the rule text, not just the linter output. An instruction file (for example, `AGENTS.md`) that restates the Priority 1 and AI-habit rules prevents most violations before the hook runs. `vale-ai-tells` recommends the same approach.\[7\]
- Scope prose rules away from code. Make sure the prose checker skips fenced code, inline code, URLs, and front matter. Run AI-12-style sentence patterns on raw text so backticked subjects don't hide matches.\[7\]
- Keep exception lists in version control: product names for sentence case (S-H3), acronyms (S-A4), and technical senses of flagged words (AI-8). Most false positives come from missing entries in these lists.
- Pin rule packages to a release. Vocabulary-based AI rules age quickly as models change, and the ammil author says it's "unclear whether these rules will be relevant or productive in a year."\[11\]\[62\]

## Caveats

- **Guide coverage.** Some Microsoft positions (present tense, "i.e.", "etc.", "please") come from search snippets of Microsoft pages rather than full-page reads. They are consistent with Microsoft's published Vale package but should be spot-checked. I found no Microsoft statement on "simply" or "easy" in the pages reviewed, and no GitLab statements on tense, "please", or exclamation marks.
- **Numbers the guides don't give.** No core guide sets a maximum list nesting depth, a sentence word count, or a heading character limit. The thresholds here (two nesting levels, 26 and 20 words, 60 characters) are house defaults, not consensus.
- **AI tells describe probability, not proof.** Every AI-habit rule also matches some human writing. The vocabulary evidence comes from biomedical abstracts, not software docs. Treat these rules as a style floor for agent output, not as authorship detection.
- **Scope.** Red Hat, Apple, DigitalOcean, and plain-language guidelines weren't compared rule by rule. Red Hat is mentioned only for its concept, procedure, and reference module types.\[63\] Adding one of them would mostly refine the word lists, not the structural rules.
- **Markdownlint rule IDs** are given as evidence of how each rule is codified. Rule names and parameters vary by version, so check them against the version you adopt.
